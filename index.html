Start here

Weeks 7 and 8 build the Riemann integral from rectangles. Week 9 returns to derivatives and ends with Taylor's theorem.

The storyline

  1. Cut \([a,b]\) into pieces. That is a partition.
  2. On each piece take the lowest and the highest value of \(f\). Add up the rectangles. That gives a lower sum \(L\) and an upper sum \(U\).
  3. Adding cut points pushes \(L\) up and \(U\) down. So every lower sum is at most every upper sum.
  4. Take the sup of the lower sums and the inf of the upper sums. If they are equal, \(f\) is Riemann integrable and that number is the integral.
  5. To prove integrability, show \(U-L<\varepsilon\) for a good partition, for every \(\varepsilon>0\).
  6. Week 9: derivatives have the intermediate value property (Darboux). The sign of \(f'\) locates extrema. Taylor polynomials approximate \(f\) with an error you can bound.

What the test looks like

Test 1 asked for one precise definition, one proof with blanks to complete, one theorem to name, one short computation and one sequence argument. Expect the same mix. The Recall drills and the Mock test train exactly that.

  • Quantifiers. Write "for every \(\varepsilon>0\) there exists \(\delta>0\)", not a loose version.
  • Domains. Write \(f:[1,2]\to\R\) when you define \(f\).
  • Theorem names. The Extreme Value Theorem (attains a max and min) and Bolzano's Intermediate Value Theorem are different results. Mixing them cost the mark.
  • Say which result you use at each step.

Pairs to keep apart

  • Lower Darboux integral \(=\sup\) of lower sums. Upper Darboux integral \(=\inf\) of upper sums.
  • An integral is a number (an area). An antiderivative is a function \(F\) with \(F'=f\). They are not the same thing.
  • Bolzano's IVT: a continuous \(f\) takes every value between \(f(a)\) and \(f(b)\). Darboux's theorem: a derivative \(f'\) takes every value between \(f'(a)\) and \(f'(b)\).
  • Absolute extremum: best on the whole domain. Local (relative) extremum: best on some small open interval.
  • The second derivative test says nothing when \(f''(x_0)=0\).

Week 7

Partitions

A partition is a way of chopping the interval \([a,b]\) into smaller intervals. You choose the cut points. Everything later (the sums, the integral) is built on a partition.

A partition \(P\) of \([a,b]\) is a finite set \(P=\{x_0,x_1,\dots,x_n\}\) with \[a=x_0<x_1<x_2<\dots<x_{n-1}<x_n=b.\] Write \(\Delta x_i = x_i-x_{i-1}\) for \(i=1,\dots,n\).

Three facts to use without thinking.

  • Both endpoints \(a\) and \(b\) belong to \(P\).
  • Each \(\Delta x_i>0\), because the points are strictly increasing.
  • The widths add up to the length. The sum telescopes: \[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta x_i=(x_1-x_0)+(x_2-x_1)+\dots+(x_n-x_{n-1})=x_n-x_0=b-a.\]

\(P\) is a set. A set has no repeated points, so the points are listed once. This is why the notes say the points are "all distinct": we need to know exactly how many pieces there are.

Your handwritten notes index the points \(x_1,\dots,x_n\) and sum from \(j=2\). The typed notes use \(x_0,\dots,x_n\) and sum from \(i=1\). Both describe the same thing. In the test, copy the convention in the question.

Uniform partitions (you must be able to build these)

Split \([a,b]\) into \(n\) equal pieces. Then \[x_i=a+i\,\frac{b-a}{n},\qquad \Delta x_i=\frac{b-a}{n}.\]

Partition \([0,3]\) into \(n=6\) equal pieces.

  1. Width: \(\Delta x=\dfrac{3-0}{6}=\dfrac12\).
  2. Points: \(x_i=i/2\) for \(i=0,\dots,6\). So \(P=\{0,\tfrac12,1,\tfrac32,2,\tfrac52,3\}\).
  3. Check: \(6\cdot\tfrac12=3=b-a\). ✓

For \([0,1]\): \(n=2\) gives \(\{0,\tfrac12,1\}\), \(n=3\) gives \(\{0,\tfrac13,\tfrac23,1\}\), \(n=4\) gives \(\{0,\tfrac14,\tfrac12,\tfrac34,1\}\).

Week 7

Lower and upper sums

On each small piece, look at how low and how high \(f\) goes. Build one rectangle whose height is the lowest value (it fits under the graph) and one whose height is the highest value (it reaches over the graph). Add up the areas. That is a lower sum and an upper sum.

Let \(f:[a,b]\to\R\) be bounded and \(P=\{x_0,\dots,x_n\}\) a partition. For \(i=1,\dots,n\) define \[m_i=\inf\{f(x):x_{i-1}\le x\le x_i\},\qquad M_i=\sup\{f(x):x_{i-1}\le x\le x_i\}.\] The lower sum and upper sum are \[L(P,f)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_i\,\Delta x_i,\qquad U(P,f)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}M_i\,\Delta x_i.\]

Why inf and sup, not min and max? On a closed piece a discontinuous \(f\) may not reach its highest value. Example: \(f(x)=x\) for \(0\le x<1\) and \(f(1)=0\) has \(\sup=1\), never attained. The inf and sup always exist because \(f\) is bounded.

Always \(m_i\le M_i\) and \(\Delta x_i>0\), so \(L(P,f)\le U(P,f)\).

  1. List the pieces and their widths \(\Delta x_i\).
  2. On each piece find \(m_i\) and \(M_i\). If \(f\) is increasing, \(m_i=f(\text{left end})\) and \(M_i=f(\text{right end})\). If \(f\) is decreasing, swap them. If \(f\) rises and falls inside a piece, look at the shape.
  3. Multiply by \(\Delta x_i\) and add.

Class example: \(f(x)=x^2-1\) on \([0,1]\)

Take \(P=\{0,\tfrac14,\tfrac12,\tfrac34,1\}\). Here \(\Delta x_i=\tfrac14\). The function \(x^2-1\) is increasing on \([0,1]\), so \(m_i\) is the left value and \(M_i\) the right value.

When the function turns inside a piece

\(f(x)=\sin x\) on \([0,\pi]\), \(P=\{0,\tfrac\pi2,\pi\}\). Each piece has width \(\tfrac\pi2\).

  1. On \([0,\tfrac\pi2]\): \(\sin\) rises from \(0\) to \(1\). So \(m_1=0\), \(M_1=1\).
  2. On \([\tfrac\pi2,\pi]\): \(\sin\) falls from \(1\) to \(0\). So \(m_2=0\), \(M_2=1\).
  3. \(L=0\cdot\tfrac\pi2+0\cdot\tfrac\pi2=0\). \(U=1\cdot\tfrac\pi2+1\cdot\tfrac\pi2=\pi\).

This partition is coarse, so the sums are far apart. The true integral is \(2\).

Why is \(L(P,f)\le U(P,f)\) for every partition?

Show answer

Show answer

Every \(m_i=M_i=c\). So both equal \(c\sum\Delta x_i=c(b-a)\), for every \(P\).

Week 7

Bounds on the sums

If \(f\) stays between \(m\) and \(M\), then the whole area under \(f\) is trapped between a short rectangle (height \(m\)) and a tall rectangle (height \(M\)). Every lower sum and every upper sum is trapped there too.

Let \(f:[a,b]\to\R\) be bounded with \(m\le f(x)\le M\) for all \(x\in[a,b]\). Then for every partition \(P\), \[m(b-a)\le L(P,f)\le U(P,f)\le M(b-a).\]

Click the grey boxes in the proof to reveal them. Try to say each one first.

Cloze proof
  1. On each piece, \(m\) is a lower bound for \(f\), so \(m\le\) \(m_i\). In the same way \(M_i\le M\). Also \(m_i\le M_i\). So \(m\le m_i\le M_i\le M\).
  2. Multiply by \(\Delta x_i\) which is positive, so the inequalities keep their direction, and add over \(i\): \[\sum m\,\Delta x_i\le\sum m_i\Delta x_i\le\sum M_i\Delta x_i\le\sum M\,\Delta x_i.\]
  3. Pull out the constants. \(\sum m\,\Delta x_i=m\sum\Delta x_i=\) \(m(b-a)\) by telescoping. Likewise the right-hand sum is \(M(b-a)\). \(\square\)

Why this matters. The set of lower sums and the set of upper sums are bounded. So their sup and inf exist. That is what lets us define the lower and upper integrals next.

Check yourself

\(f(x)=x^2\) on \([1,3]\). Use the proposition to give bounds on every \(L(P,f)\) and \(U(P,f)\).

Show answer

On \([1,3]\), \(1\le x^2\le 9\). So \(m=1\), \(M=9\), \(b-a=2\). Then \(2\le L(P,f)\le U(P,f)\le 18\).

Where exactly does the proof need \(\Delta x_i>0\)?

Show answer

When multiplying \(m\le m_i\le M_i\le M\) by \(\Delta x_i\). A negative factor would reverse the inequalities.

Why does the proposition show that "the set of lower sums is bounded"?

Show answer

Every lower sum satisfies \(m(b-a)\le L(P,f)\le M(b-a)\). So the set is bounded below and above, and has a sup.

Week 7

Lower and upper integrals

Lower sums approach the area from below. Take the best one: the biggest. Upper sums approach from above. Take the best one: the smallest. These two numbers exist for every bounded function. They may or may not agree.

For bounded \(f:[a,b]\to\R\), \[\lowint f=\sup\{L(P,f):P\text{ a partition of }[a,b]\},\qquad \upint f=\inf\{U(P,f):P\text{ a partition of }[a,b]\}.\] The first is the lower Darboux integral, the second the upper Darboux integral.

Lower integral uses sup. Upper integral uses inf. Your notes underline this. Memory hook: lower sums are small, so you want the largest one. Upper sums are large, so you want the smallest one.

If \(f\) is defined on a bigger set, use its restriction to \([a,b]\), and make sure that restriction is bounded.

Class example: the Dirichlet function

\(f:[0,1]\to\R\), \(f(x)=1\) if \(x\in\Q\) and \(f(x)=0\) if \(x\notin\Q\). It is bounded: \(0\le f\le1\).

  1. Take any partition \(P\) and any piece \([x_{i-1},x_i]\). Every interval contains a rational and an irrational (both are dense in \(\R\)).
  2. So \(m_i=0\) (an irrational gives value \(0\)) and \(M_i=1\) (a rational gives value \(1\)).
  3. \(L(P,f)=\sum0\cdot\Delta x_i=0\) and \(U(P,f)=\sum1\cdot\Delta x_i=1-0=1\), for every \(P\).
  4. So \(\lowint f=\sup\{0\}=0\) and \(\upint f=\inf\{1\}=1\).

The two integrals differ. This \(f\) will not be Riemann integrable.

Check yourself

Let \(g:[0,3]\to\R\) with \(g(x)=2\) on the rationals and \(g(x)=-1\) on the irrationals. Find the lower and upper Darboux integrals.

Show answer

Every piece has \(m_i=-1\) and \(M_i=2\). So \(L=-1\cdot3=-3\) and \(U=2\cdot3=6\) for every \(P\). Lower integral \(=-3\), upper integral \(=6\).

Write the definition of the lower Darboux integral.

Show answer

For bounded \(f:[a,b]\to\R\): \(\lowint f=\sup\{L(P,f):P\text{ is a partition of }[a,b]\}\).

Why do we take the sup of lower sums and not the inf?

Show answer

We want the best approximation from below. That is the largest lower sum. The inf would be dragged down by terrible, coarse partitions.

For constant \(f\equiv c\), what are the lower and upper integrals?

Show answer

Every \(L=U=c(b-a)\). So both integrals equal \(c(b-a)\).

Week 7

Refinements

A refinement keeps all your cut points and adds more. More cuts means rectangles hug the graph more tightly: the lower sum can only go up, the upper sum can only go down.

Let \(P\) be a partition of \([a,b]\). A refinement of \(P\) is a partition \(\tilde P\) of \([a,b]\) with \(P\subseteq\tilde P\).

On \([0,1]\) let \(P=\{0,\tfrac12,1\}\).

  • \(Q=\{0,\tfrac13,\tfrac23,1\}\) is not a refinement of \(P\). The point \(\tfrac12\) is in \(P\) but not in \(Q\).
  • \(Q'=\{0,\tfrac14,\tfrac12,\tfrac34,1\}\) is a refinement. Every point of \(P\) is in \(Q'\).
  • Any two partitions have a common refinement, their union. Here \(P\cup Q=\{0,\tfrac13,\tfrac12,\tfrac23,1\}\) refines both.

For uniform partitions: the one with \(n\) pieces is refined by the one with \(kn\) pieces. So \(n=2\) is refined by \(n=4\), but \(n=2\) is not refined by \(n=3\).

Let \(f\) be bounded and \(\tilde P\) a refinement of \(P\). Then \[L(P,f)\le L(\tilde P,f)\qquad\text{and}\qquad U(\tilde P,f)\le U(P,f).\]

Proof for the lower sum. It is enough to add one point and then repeat. Click to reveal the key lines.

Cloze proof
  1. Let \(\tilde P=P\cup\{t\}\) with \(x_{i-1}<t<x_i\). Only piece \(i\) changes. It splits into \([x_{i-1},t]\) and \([t,x_i]\). Let \(m'\) and \(m''\) be the infima of \(f\) on the two new pieces.
  2. Each new piece is inside \([x_{i-1},x_i]\). An infimum over a smaller set is larger than or equal to the infimum over the bigger set. So \(m'\ge m_i\) and \(m''\ge m_i\).
  3. The old term \(m_i\Delta x_i\) is replaced by two terms, and \[m'(t-x_{i-1})+m''(x_i-t)\ \ge\ m_i(t-x_{i-1})+m_i(x_i-t).\] The right-hand side equals \(m_i\,\Delta x_i\).
  4. All other terms are unchanged. So \(L(\tilde P,f)\ge L(P,f)\). Repeat for each added point. \(\square\)

The upper sum is the mirror image. Over a smaller set a sup is smaller or equal, so \(M',M''\le M_i\) and \(U(\tilde P,f)\le U(P,f)\).

For any two partitions \(P_1,P_2\) of \([a,b]\): \(L(P_1,f)\le U(P_2,f)\).

Proof. Let \(\tilde P=P_1\cup P_2\). It refines both. Then \[L(P_1,f)\ \le\ L(\tilde P,f)\ \le\ U(\tilde P,f)\ \le\ U(P_2,f).\] The three steps are: refine for \(L\), \(L\le U\) on the same partition, refine for \(U\). Your notes say to learn each of the three separately.

\(\displaystyle\lowint f\ \le\ \upint f\). Also \(m(b-a)\le\lowint f\le\upint f\le M(b-a)\) if \(m\le f\le M\).

Cloze proof
  1. Fix a partition \(P_2\). By the lemma, \(U(P_2,f)\) is an upper bound of every lower sum \(L(P_1,f)\).
  2. So the sup of the lower sums is at most it: \(\lowint f\le U(P_2,f)\).
  3. \(P_2\) was arbitrary. So \(\lowint f\) is a lower bound of all upper sums, hence at most their inf, which is \(\upint f\). \(\square\)

Worked numbers: watch refinement work

\(f(x)=x\) on \([0,1]\), increasing. Start with \(P=\{0,\tfrac12,1\}\) and refine to \(\tilde P=\{0,\tfrac14,\tfrac12,\tfrac34,1\}\).

\(L(P)=\tfrac12\left(0+\tfrac12\right)=\tfrac14\) and \(U(P)=\tfrac12\left(\tfrac12+1\right)=\tfrac34\).

\(L(\tilde P)=\tfrac14\left(0+\tfrac14+\tfrac12+\tfrac34\right)=\tfrac38\) and \(U(\tilde P)=\tfrac14\left(\tfrac14+\tfrac12+\tfrac34+1\right)=\tfrac58\).

\[\tfrac14\le\tfrac38\le\underbrace{\tfrac12}_{\int_0^1x\,dx}\le\tfrac58\le\tfrac34.\]

The gap \(U-L\) shrank from \(\tfrac12\) to \(\tfrac14\).

Week 8

Riemann integrable functions

A bounded function is Riemann integrable when the lowest possible area from below and the highest possible area from above meet. That single number is the integral. It is an area. It is not an antiderivative.

Let \(f:[a,b]\to\R\) be bounded. Then \(f\) is Riemann integrable if \[\lowint f=\upint f.\] The set of Riemann integrable functions on \([a,b]\) is \(\mathcal R([a,b])\). For \(f\in\mathcal R([a,b])\) define \[\int_a^b f=\int_a^b f(x)\,dx:=\lowint f=\upint f.\]

By definition, an integrable function is bounded.

Let \(f\in\mathcal R([a,b])\) and \(m\le f(x)\le M\) for all \(x\in[a,b]\). Then \[m(b-a)\le\int_a^b f\le M(b-a).\]

Proof. For any partition \(P\) we have \(m(b-a)\le L(P,f)\) by Week 7. Since \(\int_a^b f=\lowint f=\sup L\), also \(L(P,f)\le\int_a^b f\). So \(m(b-a)\le\int_a^b f\). Similarly \(\int_a^b f=\upint f\le U(P,f)\le M(b-a)\). \(\square\)

If \(f\in\mathcal R([a,b])\) and \(|f(x)|\le M\) for all \(x\), then \(\left|\int_a^b f\right|\le M(b-a)\).

Proof. \(-M\le f\le M\), so \(-M(b-a)\le\int_a^b f\le M(b-a)\).

An antiderivative of \(f\) is a function \(F\) with \(F'=f\). The integral \(\int_a^b f\) is a number, an area. Sometimes they are linked, but they are different objects and that link is a theorem we have not proved yet.

Week 8

Class example: constant functions

A constant function is a rectangle. Lower and upper sums are both exactly the area of that rectangle, whatever the partition. So the integral is height times width.

Let \(f:[a,b]\to\R\) with \(f(x)=c\) for all \(x\). Claim: \(f\in\mathcal R([a,b])\) and \(\int_a^bc\,dx=c(b-a)\).

  1. Take any partition \(P\). On every piece, \(f\) takes only the value \(c\). So \(m_i=\inf\{c\}=c\) and \(M_i=\sup\{c\}=c\).
  2. \(L(P,f)=\sum c\,\Delta x_i=c\sum\Delta x_i=c(b-a)\). Also \(U(P,f)=c(b-a)=L(P,f)\).
  3. Lower integral: \(\sup\{c(b-a)\}=c(b-a)\). Upper integral: \(\inf\{c(b-a)\}=c(b-a)\).

Riemann's integrability condition

The step-function trick works in general. A bounded function is integrable exactly when you can make the gap \(U-L\) as small as you like. You do not need to know the integral first.

Let \(f:[a,b]\to\R\) be bounded. Then \(f\) is Riemann integrable if and only if for every \(\varepsilon>0\) there exists a partition \(P\) of \([a,b]\) such that \[U(P,f)-L(P,f)<\varepsilon.\]

Proof of (⇒): integrable gives a small gap

Cloze proof
  1. Let \(\varepsilon>0\). Since \(\int_a^bf=\lowint f=\sup\{L(P,f)\}\), some element of that set beats the number just below the sup. So there is a partition \(P_1\) with \(L(P_1,f)>\int_a^bf-\)\(\varepsilon/2\).
  2. Since \(\int_a^bf=\upint f=\inf\{U(P,f)\}\), there is a partition \(P_2\) with \(U(P_2,f)<\int_a^bf+\varepsilon/2\).
  3. Let \(P=\) \(P_1\cup P_2\). It refines both, so \(L(P,f)\ge L(P_1,f)\) and \(U(P,f)\le U(P_2,f)\).
  4. Then \[U(P,f)-L(P,f)<\left(\int_a^bf+\tfrac\varepsilon2\right)-\left(\int_a^bf-\tfrac\varepsilon2\right).\] The right-hand side equals \(\varepsilon\). \(\square\)

Proof of (⇐): small gaps give integrable

Cloze proof
  1. Suppose for every \(\varepsilon>0\) some \(P\) has \(U(P,f)-L(P,f)<\varepsilon\).
  2. We always have \(L(P,f)\le\lowint f\le\upint f\le U(P,f)\). So \[0\le\upint f-\lowint f\le U(P,f)-L(P,f)<\varepsilon.\]
  3. A non-negative number smaller than every positive number is zero. So \(\lowint f=\upint f\) and \(f\) is Riemann integrable. \(\square\)

Two errors of size \(\varepsilon/2\) (one from the lower side, one from the upper side) add to \(\varepsilon\). That is the only reason for the halves.

Notation conventions

  • Restriction. If \(f\) is defined on a bigger set \(S\) and \([c,d]\subseteq[a,b]\subseteq S\), we say \(f\) is Riemann integrable on \([c,d]\) when the restriction \(f|_{[c,d]}\) is. Then \(\int_c^df:=\int_c^df|_{[c,d]}\).
  • Reversed limits. If \(b<a\) and \(f\in\mathcal R([b,a])\), define \(\int_a^bf:=-\int_b^af\).
  • Equal limits. If \(f\) is defined at \(a\), define \(\int_a^af:=0\).
  • Dummy variable. \(\int_a^bf=\int_a^bf(x)\,dx=\int_a^bf(s)\,ds\). The variable name does not matter. Use a different letter if \(x\) is already taken.

Darboux's theorem

A derivative can be badly behaved, even discontinuous. But it cannot jump over a value. If \(f'\) is below \(y\) at one end and above \(y\) at the other, then \(f'\) equals \(y\) somewhere in between. This is the intermediate value property, for the derivative.

Let \(f:[a,b]\to\R\) be differentiable. Suppose \(y\in\R\) satisfies \(f'(a)<y<f'(b)\) or \(f'(a)>y>f'(b)\). Then there exists \(c\in(a,b)\) with \(f'(c)=y\).

Bolzano's IVT is about the values of a continuous \(f\). Darboux's theorem is about the values of \(f'\), and assumes only that \(f\) is differentiable. Darboux does not assume \(f'\) is continuous.

The picture behind the proof

Here \(g\) rises at \(a\), falls at \(b\), so its maximum is strictly inside \((a,b)\). At that interior maximum the derivative is \(0\).

Proof (case \(f'(a)<y<f'(b)\))

Cloze proof
  1. Define \(g(x)=yx-f(x)\). Then \(g'(x)=\) \(y-f'(x)\). So \(g'(a)=y-f'(a)>0\) and \(g'(b)=y-f'(b)<0\).
  2. \(g\) is differentiable, so continuous on \([a,b]\). By the Extreme Value Theorem, \(g\) attains a maximum at some \(c\in[a,b]\).
  3. Not at \(a\). \(g'(a)>0\) is a limit of difference quotients, so some difference quotient is positive: there is \(x>a\) with \(\dfrac{g(x)-g(a)}{x-a}>0\). So \(g(x)>g(a)\), and \(a\) is not the maximum.
  4. Not at \(b\). \(g'(b)<0\) gives \(x<b\) with \(\dfrac{g(x)-g(b)}{x-b}<0\), so \(g(x)>g(b)\). So \(b\) is not the maximum.
  5. Therefore \(c\in(a,b)\). At an interior maximum \(g'(c)=0\), so \(y-f'(c)=0\), that is \(f'(c)=y\).
  6. If \(f'(a)>y>f'(b)\), use \(g(x)=f(x)-yx\) instead. \(\square\)

Class-style use: a derivative cannot jump

Claim: \(h:[-1,1]\to\R\) with \(h(x)=0\) for \(x<0\) and \(h(x)=1\) for \(x\ge0\) is not the derivative of any function.

  1. Suppose \(h=F'\) with \(F\) differentiable on \([-1,1]\). Then \(F'(-1)=0\) and \(F'(1)=1\).
  2. Take \(y=\tfrac12\). It lies strictly between \(0\) and \(1\).
  3. Darboux gives \(c\in(-1,1)\) with \(F'(c)=\tfrac12\). But \(h\) only takes the values \(0\) and \(1\). Contradiction.

First derivative test

If \(f\) falls up to \(c\) and rises after \(c\), then \(c\) is the bottom of a valley. If it rises then falls, \(c\) is the top of a hill. You do not even need \(f\) to be differentiable at \(c\).

Let \(f:D\to\R\). An absolute maximum is a value \(f(x_0)\) with \(x_0\in D\) and \(f(x)\le f(x_0)\) for all \(x\in D\). \(f\) has a local maximum at \(x_0\) if \(f(x_0)\) is an absolute maximum of the restriction of \(f\) to some open interval \(I\subseteq D\) containing \(x_0\). Minima are defined the same way with \(\ge\).

Let \(f:(a,b)\to\R\) be continuous, \(c\in(a,b)\), and suppose \(f\) is differentiable on \((a,c)\) and on \((c,b)\) (not necessarily at \(c\)).

  1. If \(f'(x)\le0\) on \((a,c)\) and \(f'(x)\ge0\) on \((c,b)\), then \(f\) has an absolute minimum at \(c\).
  2. If \(f'(x)\ge0\) on \((a,c)\) and \(f'(x)\le0\) on \((c,b)\), then \(f\) has an absolute maximum at \(c\).

To get a local extremum, restrict \(f\) to a small interval \((c-\delta,c+\delta)\) and apply the test there.

Proof of (i)

Cloze proof
  1. By the Mean Value Theorem, \(f'\le0\) on \((a,c)\) makes \(f\) decreasing on \((a,c)\). Likewise \(f'\ge0\) on \((c,b)\) makes \(f\) increasing on \((c,b)\).
  2. Take \(x\in(a,c)\) and a sequence \(y_n\) with \(x<y_n<c\) and \(y_n\to c\). Since \(f\) is decreasing, \(f(x)\ge f(y_n)\) for all \(n\).
  3. \(f\) is continuous at \(c\), so \(f(y_n)\to\) \(f(c)\). Taking limits in \(f(x)\ge f(y_n)\) gives \(f(x)\ge f(c)\).
  4. Take \(x\in(c,b)\) and \(y_n\) with \(c<y_n<x\), \(y_n\to c\). Since \(f\) is increasing, \(f(x)\ge f(y_n)\to f(c)\). So \(f(x)\ge f(c)\).
  5. So \(f(x)\ge f(c)\) for every \(x\in(a,b)\). That is an absolute minimum at \(c\). \(\square\)

Part (ii) is the same with the inequalities reversed.

The limit step uses continuity at \(c\). Without it the test fails. Example: \(f(x)=x^2\) for \(x\ne0\) and \(f(0)=1\) has \(f'\le0\) on the left and \(f'\ge0\) on the right, yet \(0\) is a local maximum. The problem is the jump at \(0\).

Worked examples

No derivative at \(c\). \(f(x)=|x|\) on \((-1,1)\), \(c=0\). On \((-1,0)\), \(f(x)=-x\), so \(f'=-1\le0\). On \((0,1)\), \(f'=1\ge0\). \(f\) is continuous. So \(f\) has an absolute minimum at \(0\), although \(f'(0)\) does not exist.

Local extrema. \(f(x)=x^3-3x\), so \(f'(x)=3(x-1)(x+1)\).

  1. Near \(c=1\), use \((0,2)\). On \((0,1)\): \(f'<0\). On \((1,2)\): \(f'>0\). So \(f|_{(0,2)}\) has an absolute minimum at \(1\). That is a local minimum, \(f(1)=-2\).
  2. Near \(c=-1\), use \((-2,0)\). On \((-2,-1)\): \(f'>0\). On \((-1,0)\): \(f'<0\). So a local maximum at \(-1\), \(f(-1)=2\).

If \(f\) is differentiable at an interior extremum \(c\), then \(f'(c)=0\). The first derivative test is what you use when \(f'(c)\) may not exist.

Week 9

Taylor's theorem

Near a point \(x_0\), a smooth function looks like a polynomial. The Mean Value Theorem gave the constant approximation \(f(x_0)\) with an error. Taylor adds more terms (slope, curvature, and so on) and gives an explicit error term.

Notation: \(f^{(n)}\) is the \(n\)th derivative. If \(f^{(n)}\) exists, \(f\) is \(n\) times differentiable.

For \(f\) \(n\) times differentiable near \(x_0\), the \(n\)th order Taylor polynomial at \(x_0\) is \[P_n^{x_0}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{f^{(k)}(x_0)}{k!}(x-x_0)^k=f(x_0)+f'(x_0)(x-x_0)+\frac{f''(x_0)}2(x-x_0)^2+\dots+\frac{f^{(n)}(x_0)}{n!}(x-x_0)^n.\]

Suppose \(f:[a,b]\to\R\) has \(n\) continuous derivatives on \([a,b]\) and \(f^{(n+1)}\) exists on \((a,b)\). Let \(x_0\ne x\) in \([a,b]\). Then there is a point \(c\) strictly between \(x_0\) and \(x\) with \[f(x)=P_n^{x_0}(x)+\frac{f^{(n+1)}(c)}{(n+1)!}(x-x_0)^{n+1}.\]

  • The last term is the remainder \(R_n^{x_0}(x)=f(x)-P_n^{x_0}(x)\). This form is the Lagrange form.
  • \(n=0\) gives \(f(x)=f(x_0)+f'(c)(x-x_0)\), the Mean Value Theorem.
  • The point \(c\) is unknown. It depends on \(x\) and \(x_0\). So you bound the remainder: if \(|f^{(n+1)}|\le K\), then \(|R_n|\le\dfrac{K|x-x_0|^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}\).
  • The proof is omitted in class. Know the statement, including the hypotheses.

Taylor series. If \(f\) is infinitely differentiable, the series \(\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{f^{(k)}(x_0)}{k!}(x-x_0)^k\) may not converge for any \(x\ne x_0\). Even if it converges, it need not converge to \(f\). Functions whose Taylor series at every point converges to \(f\) near that point are analytic.

  1. Derivatives of \(\sin\): \(\sin,\cos,-\sin,-\cos,\sin,\dots\). At \(0\) the values are \(0,1,0,-1,0,1,\dots\).

  3. Error bound. Use \(n=2\): \(R_2=\dfrac{f'''(c)}{3!}x^3=\dfrac{-\cos c}{6}x^3\). Since \(|\cos c|\le1\), \(|\sin x-x|\le\dfrac{|x|^3}6\).

Second derivative test

A flat spot where the graph curves upward is a valley bottom. Taylor's theorem makes this exact: near \(x_0\), \(f(x)-f(x_0)\) is a positive number times \((x-x_0)^2\).

\(f(x_0)\) is a strict relative minimum if there is \(\delta>0\) with \((x_0-\delta,x_0+\delta)\subseteq D\) and \(f(x)>f(x_0)\) for all \(x\) in that interval with \(x\ne x_0\). A strict relative maximum is the same with \(f(x)<f(x_0)\).

Let \(f:(a,b)\to\R\) be twice continuously differentiable, \(x_0\in(a,b)\), \(f'(x_0)=0\) and \(f''(x_0)>0\). Then \(f\) has a strict relative minimum at \(x_0\). If instead \(f''(x_0)<0\), it is a strict relative maximum.

Proof

Cloze proof
  1. \(f''\) is continuous at \(x_0\) and \(f''(x_0)>0\). So there is \(\delta>0\) with \(f''>0\) on \((x_0-\delta,x_0+\delta)\).
  2. Take \(x\) in that interval, \(x\ne x_0\). By Taylor's theorem with \(n=1\) there is \(c\) between \(x_0\) and \(x\) with \[f(x)=f(x_0)+f'(x_0)(x-x_0)+\frac{f''(c)}{2!}(x-x_0)^2.\]
  3. \(f'(x_0)=0\), so the middle term is zero. Hence \(f(x)=f(x_0)+\dfrac{f''(c)}{2}(x-x_0)^2\).
  4. \(c\) lies in the interval, so \(f''(c)>0\). Also \((x-x_0)^2>0\) because \(x\ne x_0\). So \(f(x)>f(x_0)\). \(\square\)

The test says nothing. \(x^4\), \(-x^4\) and \(x^3\) all have \(f'(0)=f''(0)=0\). The first is a minimum, the second a maximum, the third neither. Use the first derivative test instead.

Worked examples

\(f(x)=x^3-3x\). Then \(f'(x)=3x^2-3\) and \(f''(x)=6x\). Critical points: \(f'(x)=0\) at \(x=\pm1\).

  • \(f''(1)=6>0\). Strict relative minimum at \(x=1\).
  • \(f''(-1)=-6<0\). Strict relative maximum at \(x=-1\).

This agrees with the first derivative test result in the previous topic.

\(f(x)=\cos x\) at \(x_0=0\). \(f'(0)=-\sin0=0\) and \(f''(0)=-\cos0=-1<0\). So \(\cos\) has a strict relative maximum at \(0\).

Properties of the integral

If \(a<b<c\), then \(f:[a,c]\to\R\) is Riemann integrable iff \(f\) is integrable on \([a,b]\) and on \([b,c]\). In that case \(\int_a^cf=\int_a^bf+\int_b^cf\).

If \(f\in\mathcal R([a,b])\) and \(\alpha\in\R\), then \(\alpha f\in\mathcal R([a,b])\) and \(\int_a^b\alpha f=\alpha\int_a^bf\).

The proof splits into cases. Your notes tell you the exam may ask the case \(\alpha=-1\).

  1. On each piece, \(\sup\{-f(x)\}=-\inf\{f(x)\}\). So \(U(-f,P)=-L(f,P)\).
  2. Then \(\upint(-f)=\inf\{U(-f,P)\}=\inf\{-L(f,P)\}=-\sup\{L(f,P)\}=-\lowint f\).
  3. Since \(f\) is integrable, \(\lowint f=\int_a^bf\). So \(\upint(-f)=-\int_a^bf\).
  4. Likewise \(\lowint(-f)=-\upint f=-\int_a^bf\). Upper and lower agree, so \(-f\) is integrable and \(\int_a^b(-f)=-\int_a^bf\).

For \(\alpha\ge0\) the same idea gives \(U(\alpha f,P)=\alpha U(f,P)\), because a positive constant passes through the sup.

If \(f,g\) are bounded, then \(\upint(f+g)\le\upint f+\upint g\) and \(\lowint(f+g)\ge\lowint f+\lowint g\). If \(f,g\in\mathcal R([a,b])\), then \(f+g\in\mathcal R([a,b])\) and \(\int(f+g)=\int f+\int g\). If \(f\le g\) then \(\int f\le\int g\).

State the bounds \(m(b-a)\le L\le U\le M(b-a)\) precisely.

Define a refinement. State what refining does to \(L\) and \(U\).

\(\tilde P\) is a refinement of \(P\) if \(P\subseteq\tilde P\). Then \(L(P,f)\le L(\tilde P,f)\) and \(U(\tilde P,f)\le U(P,f)\).

Define Riemann integrable and \(\int_a^bf\).

A bounded \(f:[a,b]\to\R\) is Riemann integrable if \(\lowint f=\upint f\). The common value is \(\int_a^bf\).
